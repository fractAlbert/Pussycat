About

The maker

Pussycat was a brand of Breiter Walter GmbH & Co. KG, a plastics manufacturer in Schötmar, part of Bad Salzuflen in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany. The company is listed in local directories under plastic products rather than toys, which fits the puzzles themselves: thin moulded plastic trays with printed sliding tiles, cheap to produce and sold as novelties and gifts.

Puzzles carry Pussycat and Made in Germany on the back, often with an article number such as 80 2971 or 802931. Some later Escher pieces are marked Holland as well as Germany.

The art puzzles' backs say more. They show a one-colour print of the picture, its title (in German, often with French or English), the museum that holds it, the article number, a © VG Bild-Kunst, Bonn credit with a year, and a maker line naming Pussycat, 32075 Bad Salzuflen and, on some, W. Breiter GmbH & Co. KG.

What they made

The catalog is broader than the art series. Alongside the paintings there are crossword designs, children's motifs, and promotional puzzles printed for companies — a Lufthansa apple among them. The sliding puzzle was the house format, and almost anything could be printed on it.

There was a licensed Disney line too, sold as large puzzles: Micky Maus, Donald Duck, Goofy and Pluto have all turned up from one German seller. Some listings carry the sub-brand Games 'N Arts alongside the Pussycat name, which may be how the art series in particular was sold.

Pussy

Some sliders are marked Pussy or Original Pussy instead — German, mostly listed as 1970s and early 1980s (one as the 1990s), in the same moulded format: a long chocolate bar, an ice cream, a promotional puzzle for the rare-earths company Molycorp, an optical illusion, a pig, a Joker playing-card design, an Egyptian Tut-anch-Amun. The backs of the Joker and the Tut-anch-Amun print article numbers in the same 80 29xx series as Pussycat (80 2925 and 80 2955), which points to one numbering system and so probably one maker. Whether Pussy was an earlier name for the same maker or a separate one is still not confirmed, so the catalog records the brand on each entry and lets you filter by it.

Still in production

The brand did not stop with the vintage puzzles. Current listings offer Collector's Edition boxes of six mini sliding puzzles, each about 92 × 75 mm, on themes such as Cats, Circus, Zoo, Bears, Comics and Otmar Alt. One of them is titled World Flags 2026, so the line is being added to now rather than cleared from old stock.

The art series

The best-known line reproduces well-known paintings and prints. Artists seen so far include M.C. Escher, Wassily Kandinsky, Gustav Klimt, Vincent van Gogh, Pablo Picasso, René Magritte, Joan Miró, August Macke, Paul Cézanne, Auguste Renoir, Claude Monet, Paul Klee, Franz Marc, Salvador Dalí, and the contemporary German painter Otmar Alt.

Several artists appear more than once. Escher has a dozen designs and Miró seven, and Cézanne, Klee, Magritte, Marc, Macke, Monet, Renoir and Van Gogh each have at least four. The run is wider than a handful of greatest hits — it reads like a licensed programme working steadily through a picture library.

The Escher puzzles are the most commonly found today. Listings describe them as produced for the Escher heirs, dated around 1990, with the original print reproduced on the back of the packaging. Typical dimensions are about 15 × 12.5 cm.

Article numbers

Most puzzles carry an article number, which is the nearest thing to a catalogue key. Two families are visible so far. The 80 29xx numbers belong to 49-tile 7×7 puzzles — wildlife, dinosaurs, crosswords — and to the Pussy-marked ones. The 80 23xxx numbers belong to the fine-art run that mostly carries a 1999 copyright (the owned Picasso is marked 1996). Escher puzzles instead use an E nnn reference from the Escher catalogue raisonné.

Within the art series the numbers come in blocks, one per artist: Kandinsky 80 234x, Magritte 80 2923–2924, Macke 80 293x, Miró 80 299x, and in the longer family Klee 80 2315x, Picasso 80 2318x, Klimt 80 2319x, Monet 80 2321x and Cézanne 80 2324x. Franz Marc's are printed with different spacing, 80 29 123 and 80 29 125. The same number is printed with different spacing from puzzle to puzzle, so compare them without the spaces. One number is contested: 80 2931 is printed on the back of a Macke, and two crossword listings quote it too.

Formats

Two layouts turn up. Most art puzzles are described as 49 tiles in a 7×7 arrangement — the image is complete, and a spare cell in one corner is what lets the tiles move at all. Others follow the classic layout, where the blank is a missing tile inside the picture: the Puddle puzzle is listed as 55 sliding pieces plus a place holder.

Sellers list many of the 80 23xxx art puzzles at 62 tiles, while the Cézanne Mardi Gras from that same run measures 7×9 fully tiled with a spare corner cell, which is 63. They are not miscounts: the two are different layouts. Mardi Gras has the spare cell outside the picture; the Kandinsky Farbstudie has a gap inside it, so 62 tiles.

Ten more puzzles, from the Cézanne, Renoir, Monet, Van Gogh and Macke entries, have had their grids counted from sellers' photographs: nine show the 7×9 layout with a gap inside the picture, and the Macke is 7×7 with a spare cell. These counts are marked as photo-based in each entry, because the bottom row is sometimes out of frame; they let the puzzle be played here, and a count from the puzzle in hand would settle them.

Dates

Examples are offered as 1970s through 1990s, with individual puzzles dated 1990, 1991, and 1996. Directory entries for the company suggest it did not continue past around 2000.

About this catalog

Entries are compiled from sale listings and auction archives. Anything not yet checked against the physical puzzle is marked as such in its detail view, and each entry links to the listing it came from. Tile counts, article numbers, and titles taken from listings are frequently approximate — sellers describe the same puzzle several different ways. Where a listing shows the back of the puzzle, the title, museum and article number printed there are used instead of the listing's own: one listing's Monet turned out to be a different painting.

Corrections are welcome, particularly from anyone holding these in hand.
